# MP2 Reflection — SafeWalk UW

## What did you build?

SafeWalk is a nighttime safety companion for University of Washington students walking home through the U-District after late classes or campus work. It has two parts: a Python data pipeline that pulls real Seattle Police Department crime records and OpenStreetMap street data to score every walkable corridor on a 0–100 safety scale, and a mobile-first web app (built with Lovable) that displays those scores on a Google Maps interface with walking directions, live GPS alerts, walking-buddy groups organized by major, Waze-style incident reporting, and one-tap emergency contacts for 911, UW SafeCampus, and Husky NightWalk. The pipeline computes a composite corridor score that combines crime density, street lighting, path type, and wheelchair accessibility, then writes the results as GeoJSON files that the app loads as a map overlay. A vanilla HTML/Leaflet prototype provides a lightweight fallback that uses the same data.

## What decisions did you make?

I chose Lovable as the frontend platform because it let me move quickly from UI concept to working prototype without deep React or deployment experience — I could focus on the safety logic and data pipeline rather than build tooling. For the data source, I used the Seattle Open Data Socrata API rather than a static CSV download so the pipeline can be re-run for fresh data. I scoped the crime filter to nighttime person-crimes (assault, robbery, harassment, rape) rather than all offenses because property crimes like car theft do not directly affect pedestrian safety. The corridor score formula weights crime density most heavily (60%) but also penalizes unlit streets, stairs, and rough surfaces because those factors compound risk at night. I built the demo GeoJSON as a lighter subset (segments with at least one data attribute) to keep the map responsive, since the full file is 19 MB and over 32,000 segments. I also added nine community hotspots sourced from r/udub posts as qualitative data that official records miss — places students actually warn each other about.

## What would you do differently?

First, I would replace localStorage with a real backend (Firebase or Supabase) so that buddy groups, incident reports, and session data persist across devices and are shared among users. Right now every user sees only their own reports, which limits the tool's usefulness as a community safety resource. Second, I would integrate the UW Husky NightWalk schedule and coverage area directly into the navigate screen so the app could suggest "NightWalk is available for this route until 1:30 AM" instead of just listing a phone number. That connection between institutional safety resources and route-level data is the most valuable feature SafeWalk could offer and the piece I ran out of time to build.

## What does this work demonstrate?

This project spans multiple competency domains. The data pipeline demonstrates C2 (Collect) and C3 (Analyze): pulling live data from two APIs, filtering and joining it spatially, and computing a multi-factor safety score. The corridor-score formula and accessibility flagging demonstrate C5 (Evaluate): making explicit, documented judgments about how to weight different risk factors. The app's map layers, live alerts, and color-coded corridors demonstrate C6 (Communicate): translating a dense GeoJSON dataset into actionable, glanceable safety information. The overall system — from problem framing (C1) through building the full app (C4) to this reflection (C7) — demonstrates the ability to take a real-world safety concern, find and process public data, and deliver a working tool that a student could open on their phone while walking home at night.
